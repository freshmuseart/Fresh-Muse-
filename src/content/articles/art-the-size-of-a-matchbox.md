There is a collecting niche where the entire acquisition fits in your palm — and where price has
almost nothing to do with size.

## Why artists go tiny

Miniature work forces a kind of honesty. There is no room for the gesture to hide behind: every
line is load-bearing, every material decision visible. For editions, it also solves a practical
problem — an artist can make forty pieces in the time a large work takes to dry, without
compromising the idea.

The result is one of the last corners of the art market where excellent work still costs less
than a good dinner.

## The intimacy test

Small objects change the collector's behaviour. You do not hang a matchbox sculpture; you
shelve it, and then — this is the point — you pick it up. Art that can be held is art you
develop a relationship with through the hand, which is a rarer sense in collecting than it
should be.

It also makes rotation easy. A shelf of micro-works can be re-curated in a minute, which keeps
a domestic collection feeling alive rather than installed.

## What to look for

- **Magnification.** If the piece doesn't reward a loupe, it may only be small, not precise.
- **Housing.** Tiny work survives in good cases; ask what is included.
- **Edition size.** Small does not mean scarce — check the numbers like any other edition.
- **The artist's reason.** The best micro-work has a conceptual reason to be this size, not
  just a convenient one.

Start with one piece you can carry in a coat pocket for a week. You will know very quickly
whether this scale of collecting is for you.
