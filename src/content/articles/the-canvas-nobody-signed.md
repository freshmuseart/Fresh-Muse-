It measures 41 by 33 centimetres. A coastal interior, late afternoon, a table with fruit that
has clearly been looked at closely by whoever painted it. For eighty years it has carried three
different attributions and no signature.

## Three names, one picture

It was a "follower of" in the 1940s, a specific master in the 1970s, and by 2003 — after a
scrub of the varnish revealed a second, older sky underneath — simply "Unknown, Northern
European, early nineteenth century."

Each renaming came with a tenfold swing in value. Each renaming also said more about the
market's appetite than about the canvas itself.

## The case for staying unknown

There is a quiet argument to be made that anonymous pictures are the last ones in the art world
that you must look at rather than recognise. No name arrives with a catalogue raisonné, a
provenance drama or a ceiling price. You stand in front of the work with nothing but the work.

Curators sometimes prefer this. The moment a name attaches, the painting begins to perform its
own fame; before that, it can only perform its subject.

## What the canvas actually shows

The second sky — the one under the varnish — is better than the first. Someone revised their
mind about evening light and had the honesty to paint over themselves. There is no signature,
but there is something rarer: evidence of a decision.

We will probably never know who painted it. It hangs now with a label that says only where it
came from and when. It is, by some distance, the quietest and best thing in its room.
