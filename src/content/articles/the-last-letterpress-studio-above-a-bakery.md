The building smells of bread on the ground floor and ink on the first. Neither has changed its
methods in decades.

## Machines that remember

The centrepiece is a 1957 proof press, heavy enough that the floor beneath it has settled into a
slight permanent bowl. Its operator, Ren, learned to read its moods the way mechanics once read
engines: by the sound of the impression, by how the rollers argue with humidity.

Nothing here is fast. A run of two hundred cards takes the better part of a day, and most of
that day is setup — locking the type, mixing the colour by eye, pulling proofs until the bite is
right.

## Why bread matters

Ren chose the bakery floor deliberately. "Baking and printing are the same problem," he says.
"You are managing a material that will not wait, using machines older than you, for people who
could get it faster elsewhere."

The parallel is more than charm. The bakery's regulars became the print shop's first clients;
wedding invitations went out with the sourdough. A local economy assembled itself around two
trades that had no business being neighbours.

## What survives

Letterpress persists not because it is superior — for most jobs it plainly is not — but because
it produces an object with an edge you can feel. The impression is physical: paper bruised
gently into shape.

In an age when everything arrives weightless, that small violence has become the point.
