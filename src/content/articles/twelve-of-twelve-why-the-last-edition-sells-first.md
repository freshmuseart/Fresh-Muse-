Ask a printmaker how an edition sells and you will usually hear the same odd story: the last one
goes first.

## The numbering is a contract

When an artist writes *12/12* in pencil beneath an image, they are not merely counting. They are
making a promise about scarcity that has no legal force and enormous social force. The number
says: after this, the image will not exist in this form again.

Which is why collectors watch the denominator more closely than the subject.

## Why collectors chase the final number

There is a folk logic in collecting that the last impression carries something of the process —
that by the twelfth pull the printer's hand has fully understood the plate. It is not strictly
true, and nobody really believes it. But stories like this are load-bearing in any market built
on attention rather than utility.

The first number, meanwhile, is the traditionalist's choice: the proof closest to the artist's
original state. Neither position is wrong. They are simply two different ways of buying a
relationship with time.

## What to actually look at

If you are buying an edition for the first time, ignore the numbers for a moment and check:

- **The signature and date** — unambiguous, in the same hand as the numbering.
- **The paper** — deliberate, not merely adequate.
- **The plate mark or edge** — evidence the work was made *for* this format.
- **The certificate** — not proof of value, but proof of origin.

Editions are one of the few areas in the art world where a modest budget still buys genuinely
good work by artists who will matter later. The trick is buying something you would happily live
with if the market forgot about it entirely.

That is the only test that holds.
