In a small oil painting in Brussels, above a convex mirror hanging on the wall, a line of
elegant script reads: *Johannes de eyck fuit hic. 1434* — Jan van Eyck was here.

## A signature hiding in plain sight

The Arnolfini Portrait is famous for many things, but its slyest trick is the witness
signature. Van Eyck was not the subject, not the patron, and — as the scrawling hand in the
mirror suggests — apparently the man who walked into the room at exactly the right moment.

Painted into the convex glass, distorted by its curve, is a small figure in red: the painter
himself, attending his own transaction. The mirror becomes both self-portrait and alibi.

## Centuries of turning around

The gesture repeated itself whenever artists got restless. Dürer painted himself at
twenty-eight with the calm of a banknote portrait and again at twenty-eight with a thorn
collar's intensity. Rembrandt made a career of it — something over seventy self-portraits,
functioning as diary, résumé and mirror in equal measure.

The impulse is unchanged: when there is no one else in the studio, the nearest subject is the
person holding the brush. What van Eyck added was the wink — the implication that presence
itself was worth documenting.

## The five-second version

Modern self-portraiture is usually told as a story about phones. The truer line runs through
the oil: a convex mirror in a Flemish room, a painter's handwriting, and the oldest flat-lay
in existence.

Jan van Eyck was here. The rest is technique.
