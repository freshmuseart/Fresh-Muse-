Most rooms are decorated. This one was composed — and the difference is everything.

## One decision, then everything else

When the architect Solène Barrault took on a small apartment in the 11th arrondissement, the
client had exactly one strong instruction: a large, mostly grey 1970s canvas had to live in the
main room. Everything else, she said, could be decided around it.

Barrault took this more literally than most architects would. The painting's particular grey —
a cool grey with a thread of green running through it — became the reference for the plaster on
the walls. The floor was chosen a half-shade warmer, so the room would not go cold. The only
saturated colour in the entire apartment is the ochre of a single reading chair, positioned
where afternoon light hits it at four o'clock.

## Art as thermostat

What is striking about the finished space is how little of it announces itself. There are no
gallery-white walls, no track lighting, no sense that the art is being *presented*. The
painting is simply the room's centre of gravity, and everything else has settled into orbit.

Barrault describes her method in almost meteorological terms: "I ask where the picture wants
the light to come from. Then I let the rest of the day happen around it."

## The lesson for the rest of us

You do not need a significant painting to borrow this approach. Choose one object you already
own — a textile, a photograph, a bowl — and let it settle the argument about colour, light and
placement before you buy anything else.

Rooms that begin with an object tend to feel inevitable. Rooms that begin with a shopping list
tend to feel assembled.
