Turn an old altarpiece around — if anyone will let you — and you may find the back of it more
unsettling than the front.

## The hidden side of devotion

When a panel painting was made to be bolted shut, hinged, or pressed against a wall, the painter
knew two audiences: the congregation who would see the front, and God — presumably — who would
never see it at all. What they chose to put on the reverse was often stranger for being private.

Grotesques appear there. Snakes curling around foliage. Faces that look like portraits of people
in the workshop. Occasionally, a joke: a donkey playing a lute, painted with more care than the
saint on the obverse.

## Why they survive

Ironically, concealment is preservation. An image shielded from light, smoke and veneration for
five centuries comes back to us almost exactly as it left the studio — the colours of a working
Tuesday rather than a masterpiece.

Conservators speak of the "documentation layer" — the sketches, pentimenti and stray marks
artists left where no collector would grade them. The hidden face of a painting is often the
most honest portrait we have of how it was actually made.

## Where to look

Next time you are in a medieval gallery, walk behind the works if the vitrines allow it. Museums
increasingly display reverses — the hinges, the inventory numbers, the old wood — and they tell
a second story: not what the image meant, but what the object *did*.

Nobody covered these pictures up to hide them. They covered them up to use them. That is a
different kind of intimacy, and almost nobody photographs it.
