The loveliest green in Victorian England was also its most dangerous, and nobody particularly
minded for about forty years.

## A colour with a body count

Scheele's green, and its later cousin Paris green, were arsenic-based pigments with a
luminosity no mineral rival could match. They went onto wallpaper, printed textiles, artificial
flowers, children's toys — and, inevitably, painted canvases.

The symptoms of long exposure were documented early: headaches, nausea, a lingering cough
attributed to everything but the walls. Painters' colic was an old complaint with a new
inventory of suspects. The era's most repeated anecdote has the chemist Carl Wilhelm Scheele
handling his own arsenic compound carelessly and dying young — a story that may be apocryphal,
but which nobody at the time found implausible.

## Why the risk was worth it

The answer is obvious once you see the colour: it was *vivid*. In a century of soot-darkened
rooms and gaslight, a green that held its nerve under yellow flame was worth a headache.

Trade journals published recipes for "de-arsenicated" papers with a straight face. The
industry's response to a poisonous pigment was, essentially, a better version of the poisonous
pigment.

## What the walls remembered

By the early twentieth century the colour had retreated from fashion, and later still from
memory — which is why a Victorian green can still surprise a conservator who meets it in an
unventilated room.

Every palette is an agreement with the technology of its moment, including the parts that turn
out to be mistakes. The parlour green is a reminder that colour is never only a decision about
beauty.
