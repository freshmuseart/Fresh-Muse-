A collector once showed us two bowls: identical, from the same potter, same year. One was
flawless. The other had a chip on the rim that had been mended with gold.

The flawless one, he said, was for looking at. The mended one was for living with.

## What the chip records

A mark on an object is a record of an event — a table edge, a hurried hand, a guest who was
mortified for a week. It is the only part of the bowl's history nobody can fake, because
faking it would mean also faking the years.

Conservators have a phrase for this: *evidence of use*. In museums it is catalogued with the
same seriousness as the glaze. In homes it is usually apologised for.

## The mending as authorship

The Japanese tradition of kintsugi — repairing with lacquer and gold — is often described as
embracing imperfection. The more useful reading is that the repair becomes a second artwork,
signed by whoever cared enough to keep the object alive.

The mender's line of gold is a claim: this was worth saving. No certificate of authenticity
says it louder.

## Buying with your eyes open

None of this is an argument for buying damaged work at full price. It is an argument for
deciding, in advance, what kind of life you want the object to have. A bowl that may never be
used is a different purchase from a bowl expected to carry dinner.

Collect the flaw you would want. Then let the object earn its biography — chip by chip.
