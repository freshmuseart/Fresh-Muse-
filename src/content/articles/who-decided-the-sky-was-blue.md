For most of painted history, the sky was not blue. Not because artists were careless, but
because blue was the most expensive thing they could not easily have.

## The cost of heaven

Ultramarine — ground from lapis lazuli mined in what is now Afghanistan — cost more than gold by
weight through the Renaissance. Painters' contracts survive in which patrons specify, in
writing, exactly how many grams of ultramarine a Madonna's robe may contain. Skimping was a
documented temptation.

So skies were often rendered in cheaper azurite, or left as a ground colour, or simply not
painted at all: gold, instead, standing in for a heaven that was never weather.

## A very slow agreement

Blue as we understand it — a colour of distance, water and calm — assembled gradually. The
Egyptians made it early and lost the technique. Islamic ceramics kept it alive while Europe
looked elsewhere. Japanese woodblock prints, arriving in Paris in the 1860s, dealt the West a
hand of blues it had previously reserved for holy figures.

By the time Monet was painting haystacks, blue had migrated from the sacred to the atmospheric.
It took roughly four centuries for the colour of expensive stone to become the colour of the
far distance.

## The take-away

Colour is not a property of the world; it is an agreement between materials, money and habit.
When you look at a painting's blue, you are looking at a supply chain, a theology and a fashion
all at once.

Which is a useful thing to remember: every colour we take for granted was, at some point,
someone's luxury.
