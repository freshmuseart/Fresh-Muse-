The hallway is treated as a corridor: a thing you pass through on the way to a room that
matters. This is an architectural failure of nerve.

## Transition as material

Good architects have always known that the interval between rooms is itself a room — darker,
narrower, quieter, the palate cleanser of a house. It is the one space you move through many
times a day without ever deciding to visit, which makes it the one space you absorb
unconsciously.

Art placed here works differently from art placed anywhere else. Nobody chooses to look at it,
so it has no obligation to introduce itself. It can be strange. It can be slow. By the third
week you will have read it entirely without trying.

## What to hang where you pass

The corridor rewards sequence rather than statement: a run of small works, a single long
horizontal, or one piece placed deliberately at the far end so it resolves the walk. Sightlines
matter more than wall width — you are usually viewing from four metres away, in motion, in
whatever light the ends of the house allow.

Avoid anything that needs a standing ovation. This is the space for the quiet, odd, second-look
work — the piece you would never have given a full wall anywhere else.

## The test

Stand at the entrance of your hallway and look down it. If your eye lands nowhere in
particular, you have a wall that is apologising for itself.

Hang the strangest small thing you own at the far end. Watch what three weeks of unconscious
looking does to it — and to you.
