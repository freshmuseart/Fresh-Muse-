Most painters describe their light the way sailors describe weather: as the condition that
decides everything. Ilse Marek paints in defiance of it.

## A day reversed

Marek works from eleven at night until roughly five in the morning, in a studio with the blinds
permanently drawn. Daylight, she says, flatters everything — it makes decisions too easy. Sodium
lamps and a single overhead bulb give her a narrower argument to work with: colour has to be
proved rather than assumed.

"After dark, a red is either red or it is nothing," she tells us. "In daylight it can pretend."

## What the hours change

Working at night reorganises a practice. Visits stop happening. The phone stops interrupting.
Paint dries differently — slower, she claims, though her restorer disagrees — and the work
develops a habit of being finished at the exact moment the world wakes up.

There is also the matter of looking. Marek keeps one painting on the wall of the darkened room
adjacent to the studio and glances at it only in passing — never squarely — for weeks at a
time. "If it still holds you in the corner of your eye," she says, "it is finished. If you have
to walk over to it, it isn't."

## The cost of the method

It is not a romantic arrangement. Missed dinners, a permanently adjusted body, a social life
conducted in the margins. Marek is clear that she does not recommend it — only that she
tried the alternative for four years and produced nothing she believed.

Some practices are not chosen for their comfort. They are chosen because the work only arrives
at certain hours.
