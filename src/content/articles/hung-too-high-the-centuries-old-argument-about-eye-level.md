Walk through any museum and count the pictures that sit slightly above your natural gaze. Then
walk through a home that gets art right, and feel the difference in your shoulders.

## Where the habit came from

The convention of hanging high has two parents. The first is the salon: nineteenth-century
exhibitions stacked works floor to ceiling simply because the wall ran out, and the best places
went to the biggest names — which meant the middle of the wall climbed as prestige did.

The second is furniture. Above a sideboard, a mantel or a sofa rail, the eye expects *something*,
and the something gets pushed up until it clears the furniture rather than the viewer.

Museums quietly corrected themselves decades ago. Homes, for the most part, have not.

## The number that matters

Galleries now aim for the centre of the work to sit roughly 145–155 cm from the floor — the
average human eye line. Not above the sofa back. Not aligned to the ceiling. The picture is
hung for the person, not the architecture.

Test it at home: stand with your back to the wall, relax your eyes, and mark where you are
looking. That is your line. Everything else on the wall negotiates around it.

## Why it changes a room

A picture hung correctly stops performing and starts accompanying. Conversation doesn't tilt
upward. The room reads as though it was arranged around inhabitants rather than around the
things they own.

It is the rare interior decision that costs nothing and is almost impossible to get wrong once
you know it. Measure once. Move every hook you own. The room will feel like it exhaled.
