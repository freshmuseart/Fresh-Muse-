There is a stretch of northern coastline where, most mornings, the boundary between sea and sky
simply refuses to be drawn. It is here that the sculptor Marta Oyarzún has worked for eleven
years, making objects that are largely concerned with something that cannot be kept.

## The problem of holding weather

Oyarzún's practice began, she says, with a failure. Trained in bronze in Bilbao, she spent her
twenties chasing the perfect surface — the one that would catch light the way water does. She
never found it. "Bronze is a metal that remembers heat," she told us. "It does not remember
light."

So she stopped trying to reproduce water and started studying what happens at the moment
weather becomes architecture: the way fog presses against a lighthouse, the way salt rusts a
hinge, the way a harbour wall is really just an argument with the sea that the sea eventually
wins.

## Objects that arrive slowly

Her current series, *Mareas Inmóviles* — still tides — are cast bronze forms whose surfaces have
been buried in shoreline mud for months at a time. The metal arrives back in the studio looking
less manufactured than discovered: pitted, veined, faintly green.

Nothing about them is dramatic. They are quiet to the point of being furniture. And yet stand
with one long enough and you begin to feel the thing Oyarzún is actually sculpting, which is
duration.

> "I am not making the fog. I am making the place where the fog agreed to stay."

## Why it matters

There is a strain of contemporary sculpture devoted to spectacle — works that need a warehouse
and a press release. Oyarzún's practice points the other way: toward attention so patient it
becomes a material in itself.

Her pieces will be shown in a small coastal exhibition this winter. Most of them will be the
size you could hold in two hands. That, she insists, is the point.
